What puts a drug into shortage

Everyone says shortages concentrate in cheap generic sterile injectables. Measured against the full universe of 2,149 marketed drug markets, the sterile injectable half of that sentence is emphatic and survives every control. The cheap generic half does not survive at all.

The data does not contain what it appears to contain

The risk flagged at the outset was real, and it is worse than "current-state-heavy." The shortages endpoint returns 1,628 records. Exactly 10 carry status Resolved, and eight of those are duplicate presentations of a single hydrocortisone entry. Resolution is, for practical purposes, unobservable.

This makes the file a prevalent cohort: it samples shortages that are in progress, not shortages that occurred. A shortage that began in 2019 and ended in 2020 leaves no trace whatsoever. The consequence shows up plainly in the onset series — the 2024 trough is not a quiet year, it is the year whose shortages have mostly already resolved and been purged.

Two further gaps close off the designs one would reach for first. The NDC directory carries zero past marketing-end dates — delisted products are purged, so market exits cannot be dated from it. And drugsfda retains 23,496 discontinued products but attaches no date to discontinuation.

I tested the obvious workaround — using a firm's last approved submission as a proxy for when it went dormant — against the 294 discontinuations that do carry a true date. It leads the real date 90% of the time, but with a median lead of 3.0 years and an interquartile range of 1.3 to 5.3. That is far too coarse to date an annual event study, so I did not build on it.

Sterile injectables, unambiguously

Restricting to markets that are genuinely marketed — at least one non-discontinued approval and at least one active NDC listing — 62 of 2,149 markets are in an active shortage. The form-class gradient needs no model.

Conditional on supplier count, market size, age and generic status, the injectable odds ratio is 9.88 (4.69–20.78) — an average marginal effect of +6.8 percentage points on a 2.9% base rate. It is the most robust number in this analysis: it barely moves across specifications, it strengthens when the ten highest-influence markets are dropped, and L2 penalisation leaves it intact.

The supplier-count coefficient flips sign, and that is the finding

Raw, the folk story runs backwards. Markets with more suppliers are more likely to be in shortage — for injectables, 0.8% at a single supplier against 15.7% at nine or more. Taken at face value this refutes the concentration hypothesis outright.

It doesn't. Supplier count proxies for market size, which openFDA never reports directly: big, widely used drugs attract many suppliers and are also more visible, more demanded, and more likely to be reported short. Once the number of active listings enters as a size control, the coefficient inverts.

Because the two supplier terms correlate at 0.94, the flip invites a collinearity objection. It survives one: the model can be rewritten with no loss of fit as market size plus listings per supplier — how much of the market each supplier carries. That is a concentration measure, it correlates with size at only 0.68, and it gives OR 2.45 (1.40–4.30) per doubling. Same likelihood to four decimals, no rival regressors.

Where this result is fragile

The concentration effect depends on measuring market size from current NDC listings, which a shortage could itself have shrunk. Rebuilt purely from pre-determined approval-file quantities — products ever approved per sponsor — the same concentration measure gives OR 1.20 (0.72–2.00), p = 0.47. I judge current listings the better proxy for today's demand, since approvals-ever counts decades-dead products. But the finding rests on that judgement, and a reader who disagrees should treat concentration as unresolved.

The "cheap generic" half of the claim

No generic proxy available in openFDA reaches significance once injectable status, supplier count, size and age are controlled: generic-only markets OR 0.83 (p = 0.55), any generic present OR 2.49 (p = 0.17), log ANDA sponsor count OR 1.05 (p = 0.82). The middle estimate is large but far too imprecise to lean on.

The honest statement is stronger than "not significant": price is not in these data at all. openFDA has no price, volume, or revenue field. "Cheap" is untestable here, and the generic-status proxies that do exist are silent. Age is doing some of the work that "cheap" is usually invoked for — each doubling of years since first approval carries OR 2.69 (1.54–4.71).

Duration: the naive number is wrong by a factor of two

All 62 active shortages are right-censored — none is observed to end. Their mean elapsed time is 5.64 years, and that number will be quoted. It is badly biased upward, because a snapshot of in-progress spells over-samples long ones in proportion to their length.

Under a stationary onset intensity, the observed elapsed times are backward recurrence times, whose density is S(a)/E[T]. That relation inverts: E[T] = 1 / E[1/A]. Applying it gives a corrected mean of 2.55 years and a corrected median of 1.57 years.

The correction assumes onset intensity is roughly stationary — which the onset series above gives reason to doubt. Treat 2.55 years as a lower-bound-flavoured counterweight to the naive 5.64, not as a point estimate. The gap between them is the honest measure of how much the prevalent-cohort problem matters: a factor of 2.2.

The exit experiment cannot be run on this snapshot

This was the most interesting question asked, and the answer is that the data will not support it yet. There are 440 dated discontinuation notices. Every one of them falls between April 2023 and August 2026, and 437 fall in 2025–2026 — because notices are purged once effective, exactly as resolved shortages are.

Median follow-up after a notice is 0.5 years; only 3 of 440 have a full year. There is no post-period in which to observe a subsequent shortage onset, and only 13 markets pair an exit with a later shortage from a different firm. An event study on 13 markets with six months of follow-up would be theatre.

What can be said is cross-sectional and directionally ambiguous. Markets with a discontinuation notice are in shortage 9.7% of the time against 2.4% without — a raw ratio of 4.1×. Conditional on the same controls, that falls to OR 1.69 (0.80–3.55), p = 0.17. Causality runs both ways by construction: FDA codes 16.9% of active shortages as caused by discontinuation of manufacture, so for roughly one shortage in six the exit and the shortage are the same event, not cause and effect.

What would make it identifiable

Periodic snapshots — the fix the brief anticipated. src/snapshot.py ships in this repo and appends a dated capture of the endpoint; an NDC present at t and absent at t+1 is an observed resolution with a date attached. After roughly twelve monthly runs the duration model can be re-estimated without the stationarity assumption, and exits acquire a real post-period. The first capture is already written to data/snapshots/.

What the FDA says is going wrong

Reason codes are populated for a minority of records and the largest single category is Other, so this is context rather than evidence. It is still worth noting that manufacturing-quality problems — the mechanism most often invoked in the sterile injectable story — are coded for under 5% of active shortages, while demand increases account for 23.8%.

The discrete-time hazard is reported in the repository output but not headlined here, because its estimand is compromised by the same prevalent-cohort problem: it models onset of shortages that persist to the snapshot, which entangles onset with duration. Symptomatically, the injectable hazard ratio falls from 10.75 over 2012–2026 to 3.99 when restricted to 2022–2026, where survival filtering is weaker — most of the long-window injectable effect is injectable shortages lasting longer, not starting more often.
